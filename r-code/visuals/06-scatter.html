Scatterplots and Correlations

Last updated

Sep 18, 2026

We’ll use the dataset from Levitt 1996’s study, “The Effect of Prison Population Size on Crime Rates”, published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, which you can download locally here. Each row is a state-year: a single U.S. state observed in a single year, for 14 years, from 1980 to 1993. For each state-year, the dataset records the state’s prison population and its crime rates, as well as other demographic statistics.

Scatterplots

We’re interested in whether states with higher prison populations have higher crime rates.

Note the calls in aes(), specifying the independent (x) and dependent (y) variable.

Improved readability

Interpretation

When we set the independent variable to pris and dependent variable to criv, we are asking, how does change in the prison population correlate with change in violent crime rate? Note that the interpretation changes if we switch the independent variable to criv and dependent variable to pris, but the underlying relationship doesn’t.

Scatterplots alone are not objective measures.

Substantive Interpretation of \(r\)

Across state-years from 1980 to 1993, the correlation between prison population and violent crime was positive and moderately strong (r = 0.776).

Confounding Variables (and how visualizations help us)

Every dot here is a state in a particular year — California 1980, California 1981, Utah 1980, and so on.

Suppose, hypothetically, that incarceration rates climbed across the country over these years while crime happened to be climbing too, for unrelated reasons. Then later years would sit up and to the right, earlier years down and to the left, and we’d get an upward-sloping cloud — even if, within any single year, states with bigger prison populations had no more crime than states with smaller ones.

In that case, the pattern in our plot isn’t telling us about a relationship between prison populations and crime at all. Both variables just happened to rise over the same years, and pooling the years together turns that coincidence into an upward-sloping cloud.

Adding color

Before splitting the plot up, we can get a quick read on whether time is doing any work here by mapping year to color — if the early years cluster in one corner of the cloud and the later years in another, that’s our sign that the calendar is part of what’s producing the slope.
